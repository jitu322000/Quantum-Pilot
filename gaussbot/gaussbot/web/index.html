Common mistakes & best practices

  • Start from the best geometry you actually have. A SMILES-embedded guess is a reasonable fallback, but a real 3D structure (from an X-ray structure, a previous calculation, or careful manual building) converges faster and more reliably than relying on PM6 pre-optimization to fix a bad guess.
  • For GenECP: make sure every heavy atom/transition metal is explicitly assigned an ECP, anything left unchecked falls through to the "light" basis, which is usually wrong for those elements and can fail silently rather than erroring.
  • If the IRC doesn't clearly connect to both the reactant and product, don't assume the TS is wrong, try increasing the number of points per direction first. A path that just hasn't finished relaxing looks identical to a genuinely wrong TS in this check.
  • Double-check charge and multiplicity by hand for radicals, ions, or anything not a neutral closed-shell singlet, automatic detection from a SMILES or file can get this wrong, and a wrong multiplicity silently produces a wrong (but real-looking) answer.
  • Supply your own TS guess for anything beyond the simplest reactions. The auto-generated guess is a crude linear interpolation between reactant and product, fine for simple isomerizations, unreliable for anything with major bond reorganization.
  • Right-size %nprocshared/%mem to the actual system, more isn't free, and over-requesting resources you don't have can make a job fail or queue rather than run faster.
  • The two extra reactant/product cross-checks (TS-mode distortion, IRC-endpoint reoptimization) are most useful when you're not fully sure the TS connects the right minima, they cost extra compute, so they're worth enabling especially for anything novel or unusual, less so for a well-understood reaction you've already verified before.
  • If an optimization keeps failing to converge, check the "max repair attempts" setting, a geometry genuinely close to converging (most criteria already met) often just needs more attempts, not a different starting point.
